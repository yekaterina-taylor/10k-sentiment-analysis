How to read the colors: what Strong, Adequate and Weak mean

Each value in the ratio table is shaded by comparing it with general rule-of-thumb benchmarks. The symbol in each cell gives the same information without relying on color.

Keep in mind: these are general standards, not rules. Healthy levels differ by industry and business model. For example, a very profitable company with steady cash flow can safely run a current ratio below 1.0, and heavy share buybacks raise debt-to-assets without signaling distress.

    How to read this: what each sentiment measure means and how to read the colors

    These measures come from the Loughran-McDonald dictionary, word lists built specifically for financial documents. The program counts how many words from each list appear in the MD&A. A word can be on more than one list (for example, may counts as both uncertain and weak modal).

    MeasureWhat it capturesExample words / formula ExampleHow to read it

    Most words in a 10-K are neutral (revenue, fiscal, product), so only about 2–3% of words carry sentiment. That is why sentiment scores are small numbers close to zero.

    How to read the colors

    The P/N ratio and sentiment score cells are shaded by tone. The symbol in each cell gives the same information without relying on color.

    CategoryP/N ratioSentiment scoreWhat it means
    ✓Net positive tone≥ 1.0≥ 0 Positive words equal or outnumber negative words.
    ●Typical / cautious0.5 – 1.0−0.005 to 0 More negative than positive words, which is normal for a 10-K because companies must discuss risks.
    !Strongly negativebelow 0.5below −0.005 Negative words outnumber positive words more than 2 to 1, or make up an unusually large share of the text.

    Unlike the financial ratio benchmarks, there is no standard cutoff for MD&A tone; these categories are our own. The two columns can disagree for the same year, because the sentiment score also depends on how many words in the text carry sentiment at all.
